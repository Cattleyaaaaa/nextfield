---
title: "A Transition Needs Somewhere to Land"
date: "2026-10-11"
tags: ["Transitions", "State", "Reading"]
summary: "When a book opens, a track changes or a listening mode expands, motion should explain how the old state ends and where the new one begins."
---

An interface change is often treated as a request to add an animation. If its timing and direction have little to do with the content, the result is only an attractive cover. Once it clears, the visitor must work out where they are again. A meaningful transition answers two questions first: what is being left, and what has been reached?

## Dimensions need continuity

If clicking a book cover suddenly produces a reading surface much wider than the book, even a page-turn effect cannot prevent the jump from feeling odd. The eye follows edges and centre of mass. When the edges do not meet, motion fails to explain the change. Opening from cover to contents can alter thickness, direction and light, but its final dimensions should connect to the actual reading area.

Music has a similar problem. When entering a pure listening mode, the player, artwork and lyrics may move, but the background should not disappear first and reappear from black. Keeping a shared colour or shape through the transition often creates more continuity than another overlay.

## Prepare the new state before the old one leaves

A flash is often a frame with nothing designed in it, rather than an animation that is too short. The old view unmounts; the new one has not laid out, its image has not loaded, and its colour has not been calculated. The browser exposes the base layer, and the listener experiences a black screen.

Keep a scene background present. Let new content occupy its destination while old content fades. Hold space for an image before it arrives, and let a paused visualisation settle into a still composition. These choices maintain trust during the handover.

## The ending matters as much as the entrance

Many transitions spend their energy on the opening and forget to say when the next action is available. A button looks present but cannot be clicked; a catalogue closes while background scrolling remains locked; a lyric mode opens but hides the way back. Visual completion and interactive completion should meet at the same moment.

Closing also has a direction. Opening leads from an entrance into content. Closing should return attention near that entrance rather than throwing someone to the top of the page. For visitors who prefer reduced motion, the state can change immediately, while focus and reading position still receive care.

The value of a transition is not to prove that a page can move. It is to let someone continue without stopping to ask where the previous view went or what they can do now.
