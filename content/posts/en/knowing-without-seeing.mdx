# On the Unreliability of “Knowing What You Are Doing”

We usually think that if a person knows what they are doing, they should be responsible for it. That “knowing” is treated as an obvious premise in law, morality, and everyday blame.

But what does it mean to “know what you are doing”?

A surgeon opens a patient's chest. Does the surgeon know what they are doing? Of course. But ask what that motion means in the universe, and they may know nothing. They know, “I am performing a heart bypass,” not “I am changing the whole future of a life.”

So which “what” do they know?

---

There is a crack here.

When we say “I know what I am doing,” we usually mean: **I know how society classifies my action**. I know “this is surgery,” not “this is harm”; “this is a loan,” not “this is fraud.” Once the classification is fixed, responsibility seems fixed with it.

But somebody else supplied that classification. Society, language, and habit laid the track in advance. I place my action on it, then say, “I know what I am doing.”

The question becomes: **What if the classification itself is wrong?**

---

A soldier presses a button and launches a missile. Does he know what he is doing? He knows, “I am following orders.” That category lets him sleep.

But in the destroyed house, a child was doing homework. To the child's mother, the soldier's action has a different name—one without the words “following orders.”

Two classifications, two kinds of “knowing,” one act.

Who, then, truly knows what they are doing?

---

This is not only a question of knowledge. It is a question of **who has the right to name an act**.

“Knowing what you are doing” is never accomplished alone. It requires a community, a language, and an accepted system of categories. You can say “I know” because somebody has already given the act a name.

So when someone says it, perhaps they mean only: **I accept the conventional name for this act**.

That does not mean they have really seen it.

---

Is there a kind of knowing that does not rely on ready-made categories?

Perhaps. But it is rare and uncomfortable. It asks a person, in the midst of acting, to look beyond the label and at the act itself: what it opens and closes, whom it makes laugh or cry, what it adds to or takes from the world.

This kind of knowing offers no shelter. You can no longer protect yourself with “I was only following orders,” “I was only operating,” or “I was only lending money.”

---

The real question, then, is not “Do I know what I am doing?” but:

> **Am I willing to see what I am doing?**

Knowing is a cognitive question. Seeing is an ethical one. The first can be settled with categories; the second must be borne by oneself.
