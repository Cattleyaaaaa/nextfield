When building a homepage, it is tempting to place everything you love above the fold: big type, motion, projects, music, buttons, and an ambitious declaration. Each element may be good on its own. Together, they compete to speak.

## Decide on the first sentence

The first screen's most important job is to help visitors answer: “What is this place, and why should I keep looking?” If the answer requires five paragraphs, the problem is not that the type is too small. The information has not been prioritized.

Write down the one sentence people should remember, then give it one supporting action. Projects, courses, and writing can appear in the next layer. They are not diminished; each gets its own moment to be seen.

## Motion consumes attention too

Animation is not free. If one area keeps spinning, the adjacent text becomes harder to read quietly. Let motion happen on entry or interaction, or provide an obvious way to stop it. That is often more powerful than keeping every element active forever.

## Quiet is not empty

A restrained first screen can still have a distinct character: a clear type scale, one memorable graphic, and a direct way in. White space does not mean a lack of content. It makes room for what matters most.

A good opening does not rush to tell the whole story. It makes people want to read on.
