We often draw an ideal path: open, fill in, submit, finish. Real people switch to a message, press Back, close a panel by accident, or lose the connection at the final step. A path designed only for smooth completion is not designed for real use.

## Is it still there when they return?

A draft remaining after leaving and returning to an editor is continuity. So is music that does not restart when a visitor opens another article. These experiences are rarely called “features,” but their absence is obvious.

Not every state must be saved forever. A transient tooltip can disappear; long unsent text, an ongoing task, and a deliberately chosen preference deserve to remain. The difference depends on how much effort the user has invested.

## An error page is not a dead end

An error message should answer at least two questions: What happened, and what can I do now? Retry, go back, and inspect saved work are concrete routes. “Try again later” is enough only when the system truly offers no other option.

## Interrupt the flow to test it

After designing a flow, press Back, refresh, or disconnect the network at each step. Watch what gets lost, which buttons stop working, and which states contradict each other. This reveals a product's real boundaries more readily than clicking through the happy path.

Good interaction is not impossible to interrupt. It knows the way back when interrupted.
