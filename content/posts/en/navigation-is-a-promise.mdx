An entry called “Lab” suggests there is something to try. “How It Was Made” suggests an explanation of the work. A name is not merely a label on a door; it shapes what visitors expect behind it.

## Names and content must keep each other's promises

If a link says “Start Learning” but leads to an unstructured list of related articles, visitors must guess the next step. If “View Projects” opens a page of abstract claims about skills, the promise also goes unkept.

When designing navigation, reverse the test: could a first-time visitor predict the first screen after clicking from the link text alone? The better the prediction, the less explanation the landing page needs to rescue its navigation.

## Deep content needs doors too

A page may be excellent yet rarely visited because its only link sits at the very bottom of the homepage. The answer is not to put every link in the top bar. Put entrances where they fit the visitor's intent: someone interested in process can find the build log beside a project; someone ready to practice can find exercises beside a lesson.

A page can have several entrances, but each should offer a different reason to enter. **Repeating an entrance is fine; repeating the same reason is the problem.**

## Give people a route out

Navigation includes returning. After a long article, visitors should know which section it belongs to and what to read next. After an experiment, they should see the related concept or implementation note. That makes the site feel like a map, not a set of rooms without signs.
