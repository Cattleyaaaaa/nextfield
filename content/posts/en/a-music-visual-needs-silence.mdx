Start a song and a row of bars, a glowing sphere and a rising floor can catch the eye immediately. Three minutes later, the same scene may feel crowded. Every element demonstrates that it can move, while the music slips into the background. A music page needs visual response, but that response should not be louder than the song.

## Not every frequency needs a starring role

Spectrum data changes many times a second. Mapping every sample directly to height makes a scene responsive and restless. Listening involves more than instantaneous loudness: the decay after a drum hit, the tension between a verse and a chorus, and the space left by a brief pause.

Visual changes need selection. Bass can give a broad shape a slow breath. Midrange can lift a few nearby tiles. Treble can remain in a small number of edge lights. These layers need not peak together. If they all compete for the beat, the scene loses depth.

Smoothing should not erase every difference. An attack can be quick enough for a clear beat to register; the release can take longer, allowing the scene to settle. As with a pause in a sentence, the return to rest gives the next change its weight.

## Design a shape that survives stillness

If pausing leaves a black screen, the composition depends entirely on continuous movement. A useful resting state still presents the title, artwork, lyrics and spatial relationship of the scene. The sphere keeps its outline, the tiles retain a little depth, and the colour remains tied to the current track. They simply grow quiet.

The same foundation helps during a track change. Artwork and accent colour may blend into the next selection without making the whole structure vanish and reappear. A stable setting tells the listener they are still in the same room; the work being played has changed.

## A local wave has more direction than a boiling floor

With a floor made of small blocks, moving all of them together is easy. It soon becomes a flashing surface without a point of focus. When a few blocks respond first and their neighbours follow, the grid acquires distance and sequence.

A raindrop supplies a clear origin. Tiles near the impact rise, a ripple travels outward, and its strength fades at the edge. Each tile can move only a little; the direction of travel makes the wave legible. Too many drops overlap into noise. Fewer impacts give each one room to be noticed.

A visualisation need not translate audio frame by frame. It can give listening a place: moving when movement matters, resting when it does not, and letting someone look away from the screen while the music continues.
